Volatility Surface Skew and State-Dependent Price Responses in S&P 500 Futures

Chantry Fultz · Working paper, version 1.0 (protocol v0.2), generated 2026-11-21. Status: main analysis on sampled order flow (deviation D-014). Not peer reviewed.

Abstract. We study whether option-implied downside skew conditions the short-horizon response of S&P 500 E-mini futures (ES) prices to observed order-flow pressure. Using Databento historical data for all 250 regular sessions of 2025 (250 qualifying), we build a strictly lagged 30-day 25-delta skew from consolidated SPXW option quotes every five minutes, with forwards and discount factors from put-call parity and a shape-constrained convex fit of normalized call prices. ES order flow is a 1-second sampled best-quote imbalance (a budget-driven deviation from event-level data). In the locked final period (September-December 2025, 85 sessions), the coefficient on selling pressure × standardized skew for the five-minute midquote return is 0.114 bps per standard deviation of flow and skew (95% moving-block bootstrap interval [-0.574, 0.612]; null-centered p = 0.670). The 95% block-bootstrap interval includes zero, so the primary null of no sell-pressure × skew interaction is not rejected at the 5% level. Frozen pretest forecasts show an incremental out-of-sample change 1 − SSE(M_SKEW)/SSE(M_FLOW) = -0.0034 (interval [-0.0060, -0.0009]). Estimates are conditional, observational associations; the main limitations are sampled order flow, a single year and market pair, and cross-market timing.

1. Introduction

Two minutes can show similar selling pressure in the E-mini S&P 500 futures book while downside protection in the S&P 500 options market is priced very differently. If the price of crash protection carries information about how fragile liquidity is, or about how other participants will respond to further selling, the price path after the pressure might differ between those two minutes. This paper asks whether it does. The options state is measured strictly before the pressure occurs, and the response is measured strictly after it.

The object of study is a conditional, observational price response, not a structural price-impact estimate. We do not identify the effect of an externally imposed trade, informed trading, dealer hedging or permanent changes in fundamental value. Skew here is a relative-pricing coordinate of the option surface (the implied-volatility gap between 25-delta puts and calls at a constant 30-day maturity). It is not a physical crash probability.

The design has four parts.

  1. Skew state. Every five minutes we build a 30-day surface from consolidated SPXW quotes, with forwards and discount factors estimated from the quotes themselves, and read off the 25-delta skew under two different surface constructions.
  2. Order flow. We measure best-quote order-flow imbalance (OFI) in the dated ES contract over a one-minute window. Under the project budget it is sampled at one-second resolution.
  3. Response. We relate the ES midquote return over the following one to fifteen minutes to buying and selling pressure, their interaction with the lagged skew state, and controls for volatility, spread, depth, recent returns and seasonality.
  4. Locked design. Every modelling choice was frozen at 2026-11-22 01:43 UTC (design_freeze.json, source hash a348e12a46c3), before outcomes from the final September-December 2025 period were read.

Findings.

  • Primary estimate. The sell-pressure × skew coefficient at five minutes is 0.114 bps (95% CI [-0.574, 0.612], p = 0.670).
  • Secondary family. 0 of 9 secondary interaction tests are significant after Holm adjustment.
  • Robustness. The estimate does not change materially across the second surface method, quote filters, delayed endpoints or roll exclusion.
  • Forecasting. Adding skew to the flow model does not improve frozen out-of-sample forecasts; the change in squared error is -0.0034.

We read this as a well-measured null within the precision of one year of data. The minimum detectable effect at 80% power is about 0.82 bps per SD², so small interactions cannot be ruled out.

The contribution is narrow:

  • a strictly lagged, constant-maturity skew state built consistently from option premiums;
  • a short-horizon response specification that separates the flow interval from future returns;
  • controls and interactions that challenge the idea that skew simply stands in for volatility or depth;
  • robustness to a second surface method and to quote-quality filters;
  • inference that respects day-level dependence;
  • a reproducible, budget-audited pipeline from vendor metadata to paper.

Section 2 places the question in the literature. Section 3 describes data, conventions and sample. Section 4 covers option measurement, and Section 5 covers order flow, timing, models and inference. Sections 6-8 report the association, forecasts and robustness. Sections 9-11 discuss, list limitations and conclude. The appendix contains derivations, exact formulations, the variable dictionary, the bootstrap algorithm, full tables and reproducibility details.

2. Related literature and novelty scope

Order flow and price formation. Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014) define best-quote order-flow imbalance and show that it explains most contemporaneous short-interval price changes, with a slope inversely related to depth. Cont, Cucuringu and Zhang (2023) extend the measure to multiple book levels and across assets. They find that multi-level OFI improves contemporaneous fit and that lagged cross-asset OFI has short-lived predictive content. Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters and Wyart (2004) document that price impact is partly transient, which is why we report the whole one-to-fifteen-minute horizon profile rather than a single "permanent impact".

The E-mini and state dependence. Hasbrouck (2003) finds that most S&P 500 price discovery occurs in the E-mini contract, which makes ES the natural venue for the response variable. Closest in market and spirit, Takahashi (2025, preprint) estimates a structural VAR on one-second ES best-quote data and finds that price-flow dynamics change around macroeconomic announcements. That work conditions on news events; we condition on a continuously measured option-implied state.

Option-implied downside risk and liquidity. Christoffersen, Feunou, Jeon and Ornthanalai (2021) show at daily frequency that option-implied crash risk tracks stock-market illiquidity more closely than spot variance. This is the most direct motivation for expecting skew to condition liquidity-sensitive price responses. Our study moves the question to intraday horizons and uses depth and spread as explicit competing controls.

Measurement of the surface. Ulrich and Walther (2020) show that option-implied moments depend on how the volatility surface is constructed. We therefore build skew with two different methods. Gatheral and Jacquier (2014) characterize when SVI surfaces are free of static arbitrage, and Cohen, Reisinger and Wang (2020) repair traded option prices to remove arbitrage within bid/ask bounds. Our primary method is a shape-constrained convex fit in that spirit; our robustness method is raw SVI, for which we make no global no-arbitrage claim.

Information between options and the underlying. Option markets contribute to price discovery (Chakravarty, Gulen and Mayhew 2004), and option-induced order flow predicts underlying returns (Hu 2014). Option prices are also shaped by market-maker inventory (Muravyev 2016) and respond to informed trading in the underlying (Collin-Dufresne, Fos and Muravyev 2021). The last point implies that skew can respond to ES order flow. This is why our state is strictly lagged before the flow window.

Inference. Overlapping-horizon returns and persistent regressors make naive standard errors badly optimistic (Hodrick 1992). We use a moving-block bootstrap (Künsch 1989) over whole trading-session blocks.

Novelty scope. Our search (two extended web searches, one peer-reviewed index search and reference checks; see docs/novelty_audit.md) found no study that conditions the forward ES response to OFI on a strictly lagged, constant-maturity SPXW skew state with two-method measurement robustness. That search was limited, and its result is not evidence that no such study exists. The literature review is verified at the bibliographic and abstract level; full-text reading of the closest papers is listed as future work.

3.2 Contract family and conventions

SPXW options are Cboe's weekly and end-of-month S&P 500 index options, described by Cboe as European-style, cash-settled and PM-settled, with a multiplier of 100. Cboe's product page states that "Trading in SPXW options will ordinarily cease on the day of expiration, 4:00 pm ET, and at 1:00 pm ET for any half day holiday." Standard AM-settled SPX options are excluded by root. Databento definitions confirm the root, underlying, option type and strikes in index points. They leave the multiplier and settlement style undefined (sentinel values), so those rest on Cboe's specification. Definition expiration fields are dates encoded at 00:00 UTC, not settlement instants.

The frozen protocol used 16:00 New York on the expiry date as the economic expiry instant. Section 4.6 quantifies the effect of instead using the 13:00 close for half-day expiries. Premiums and strikes stay in index points throughout; the multiplier never enters implied-volatility inversion.

3.3 Contract selection

Contracts were selected before each session from that day's definitions:

  • Expiries: the nearest PM expiries at or below and at or above 30 days across the session's five-minute cutoffs, within 14-90 days;
  • Strikes: all listed strikes within 0.85-1.15 times the previous session's ES reference close;
  • Types: calls and puts.

On average 483 contracts were requested per session (range 182-1154). Two expiries were needed on 239 sessions and three on 11. The prespecified 0.75-1.25 expansion was never needed: 25-delta roots stayed inside quoted support.

3.4 Session calendar and ES roll

The study session is the cash-index regular session, 09:30-16:00 New York, on the XNYS calendar (shortened to 13:00 on early closes). Observation endpoints run from 09:40 to 15 minutes before the close. ES is the dated quarterly contract, rolled at the start of the session on the Thursday eight calendar days before its third-Friday expiry. The 2025 rolls were: 2025-03-13 → ESM5, 2025-06-12 → ESU5, 2025-09-11 → ESZ5, 2025-12-11 → ESH6. No order flow, return or rolling state is carried across a roll; outcomes are built inside each session.

3.5 Timestamps and the sampled order-flow deviation

SPXW snapshots. For cbbo-1m, ts_recv is the clamped end of each one-minute interval, and we use it as the conservative availability time. ts_event can be a last-trade time, so it is never used as quote age. A record exists only for intervals with a quote update or trade.

ES order flow. bbo-1s records the best bid and offer at the end of each one-second interval in which the book or a trade changed. The original protocol called for event-level mbp-1. Because event-level data would have cost 7-12 times the budget, we construct a 1-second sampled OFI and label it as such throughout (deviation D-014). On the development audit day (2024-02-01, 366 minutes), sampled and event-level OFI per minute have:

  • correlation 0.92 (Spearman 0.92);
  • sign agreement 90%;
  • a sampled-to-event SD ratio of 0.63, reflecting netting within each second.

SPXW and ES are collected in different locations, so we never rely on cross-dataset nanosecond ordering. The options state is deliberately lagged instead (Section 5.2).

4. Option measurement

4.1 Parity, forwards and normalization

For each expiry and cutoff we fit C(K) − P(K) = A − BK on matched strikes, so that D = B and F = A/B. The fit is a robust Huber regression weighted by the width of each bid/ask parity interval [C_bid − P_ask, C_ask − P_bid]. An expiry needs at least four matched pairs and an implied effective rate inside (−10%, 25%).

  • Fits: 39636 expiry fits across all cutoffs; 99.99% succeeded, with a median of 79 pairs.
  • Interval violations: only 0.10% of fits violate any bid/ask parity interval.
  • Implied rates: median 4.59%, 5th-95th percentile 3.36-5.87%. These are plausible 2025 financing rates, recovered from quotes alone without an external rate curve.

With x = K/F and normalized call c = C/A (puts map via c = p + 1 − x), the measurement is invariant to any common rescaling of premiums (Appendix A).

4.2 Quote filters and selection into quotes

A quote is usable if:

  • its latest snapshot interval ended at most 120 s before the cutoff;
  • it is two-sided with positive sizes and a positive midpoint;
  • its relative spread is at most 0.50.

Per cutoff, an average of 483 selected contracts had a record, of which 466 were usable. The rest were 12.2 stale, 0.2 not two-sided and 4.7 too wide on average.

Selection into quotes. Selection does not run toward stress: the usable share is higher in high-volatility states. The usable share is 98.5% in the highest-ATM-IV quartile against 95.2% in the lowest (correlation with ATM IV 0.17). It is slightly lower late in the day (95.6% after 14:00 versus 97.7% before 11:00).

4.3 Method A: shape-constrained normalized call prices (primary)

For each expiry, Method A solves a convex quadratic program for normalized call values on the observed strike knots, constrained to:

  • price bounds max(1 − x, 0) ≤ c ≤ 1;
  • slopes between adjacent knots in [−1, 0];
  • nondecreasing slopes (convexity).

These are exactly the finite-strike static no-arbitrage conditions. The objective averages spread-normalized squared midpoint errors, adds a soft penalty for leaving each bid/ask interval and a curvature roughness penalty λ·Σ(Δs)²/Δx (Appendix C).

λ was chosen from the prespecified grid {0, 1e-4, 1e-2, 1} by grouped held-out strike-pair price reconstruction on 118 development 12:00 snapshots (1180 folds). A held-out call and its parity-equivalent put leave together, and parity is re-estimated without them. The selected value is λ = 1.0, at the edge of the grid, which was not extended. Fitted values stay essentially inside the quoted intervals (mean band violation 1.0e-08 in normalized price units).

4.4 Method B: independent raw SVI (robustness)

Method B fits raw SVI total variance w(k) = a + b[ρ(k − m) + √((k − m)² + σ²)] per expiry. It uses the same quotes and parity normalization as Method A, a spread-normalized premium loss, bounded parameters, deterministic initialization and past-fit warm starts.

Parameter bounds do not imply absence of arbitrage, so every fit is checked on a dense grid inside its observed support. Only 45.8% of SVI fits pass all finite-grid checks. In a diagnostic sample the failures were all slope violations in the far upside wing, where fitted call prices rise slightly with strike. These lie beyond the 25-delta call strike, and no convexity failures occurred. Method B is therefore a measurement-robustness check, not an arbitrage-free surface.

4.5 Constant maturity, skew and agreement between methods

Between the bracketing expiries, Method A interpolates normalized call prices at common x and Method B interpolates total variance at common k. Both are linear in elapsed maturity to exactly 30 days and stay inside common strike support with no extrapolation.

SK30 = σ(25Δ put) − σ(25Δ call) uses the undiscounted forward-delta convention: call delta Φ(d₁), put delta Φ(d₁) − 1. Each root must be unique and bracketed inside support. Across 19356 cutoffs where both methods succeed:

  • SK30: correlation 0.9984, mean absolute difference 0.071 vol points (95th percentile 0.184);
  • ATM IV: correlation 0.99994.

4.6 Measurement uncertainty and the half-day expiry instant

Quote perturbation. At calendar-chosen 12:00 snapshots (the first session of each final month plus the two pilot days), we drew 25 premium sets uniformly inside every quote's bid/ask interval and refit parity and both surfaces. Method A's SK30 moved with a standard deviation of 0.05-0.11 vol points across snapshots, against a pretest skew SD of 2.31 vol points. There were 0 failed refits (Table 6). These are measurement stress ranges, not confidence intervals.

Half-day expiry instant. A post-freeze review found that SPXW expiring on a half-day holiday stop trading at 13:00, not 16:00. Recomputing Method A states with the corrected instant for the 32 sessions whose brackets can include such an expiry changes SK30 by 0.006 vol points on average (maximum 0.126) at the 857 affected cutoffs. It also changes whether a state can be formed at 96 cutoffs. All of these are states valid under the frozen convention that become unavailable under the correction, on three sessions (2025-06-03, 2025-10-29, 2025-11-24). On those days the 30-day target falls on a half-day expiry date. With the 13:00 instant that expiry drops below 30 days in the afternoon, and the next expiry needed to bracket it was not acquired, because contract selection used the frozen 16:00 convention. We report the frozen results and document the correction (decision D-024).

5. Order flow, timing, models and inference

5.1 Sampled order-flow imbalance and controls

Between consecutive valid ES bbo-1s records n−1 and n, the Cont-Kukanov-Stoikov increment is

e = 1{P_b ≥ P_b⁻}·Q_b − 1{P_b ≤ P_b⁻}·Q_b⁻ − 1{P_a ≤ P_a⁻}·Q_a + 1{P_a ≥ P_a⁻}·Q_a⁻,

where ⁻ marks the previous record. At unchanged prices both indicators apply and the term reduces to the size change. Increments are summed over the flow window (t − 60 s, t] and divided by time-weighted mean top depth (Q_b + Q_a)/2 in contracts.

A flow window is invalid if:

  • any record gap exceeds 5 s (10 s as a sensitivity);
  • a state is flagged bad or crossed.

Segments reset at session start and after invalid states, so restoration jumps never count as flow.

Controls are:

  • 30-day ATM IV;
  • a trailing 30-minute RMS one-minute return (excluding the flow minute);
  • time-weighted relative spread and log depth;
  • the flow-minute return (a control, never an outcome);
  • prior 5- and 60-minute returns;
  • lagged OFI;
  • first and second Fourier harmonics of the session minute;
  • weekday dummies, an early-close flag, ES days to expiry and a roll-day flag.

The data dictionary is in Appendix G.

5.2 Locked information timeline

Observations are minute endpoints t. The skew state used at t is the latest valid state that satisfies:

  • cutoff s ≤ t − 120 s;
  • available by t − 60 s;
  • t − s ≤ 600 s.

States are computed every five minutes from snapshot intervals ending at or before s, and are available at s + max(5 s, measured fit time). Outcomes are ES midquote log returns over (t, t + h] for h in {1, 2, 5, 10, 15} minutes. The quote at each endpoint must be at most 5 s old. A delayed variant runs from t + 5 s to t + 5 s + h.

Example: for t = 10:00 New York, the flow window is (09:59, 10:00]. The state comes from the 09:55 cutoff (available 09:55:05), the 10:00 state is rejected as future information, and the primary outcome ends at 10:05 (Figure 1). This example is a unit test.

5.3 Models and transforms

Let q be the pretest-winsorized (0.5th/99.5th percentile) normalized OFI divided by its pretest SD, without demeaning so that zero flow stays zero. Then b = max(q, 0), s = max(−q, 0), and z is SK30 standardized with pretest mean and SD. All controls are standardized with pretest moments. Three nested models are fit on identical rows:

  • M_PRICE: y = α + Xθ;
  • M_FLOW: M_PRICE plus b, s, b·RV, s·RV, b·log depth and s·log depth;
  • M_SKEW: M_FLOW plus z, b·z and s·z.

The primary coefficient is δ_sell, on s·z, at h = 5 minutes with Method A. A negative δ_sell would mean that higher skew goes with a more negative subsequent response to selling pressure.

5.4 Inference and precision

The final calendar grid lists all 85 scheduled final sessions. Each bootstrap replication draws moving blocks of five consecutive session positions. It keeps every observation of each drawn session, relabels repeated copies and refits the regression, with 1,999 replications and seed 1729. Block boundaries create no new returns. The two-sided test is null-centered, p = (1 + #{|θ* − θ_hat| ≥ |θ_hat|})/(B + 1), and the primary interval is the basic bootstrap interval (Appendix H).

On a dependent synthetic panel (persistent skew, autocorrelated flow and errors, skew-dependent error variance), the test rejected a true null in 6.0% of 200 replications (Monte Carlo SE 1.7%). It rejected δ = −0.25 in 100.0% of replications (synthetic). With the empirical bootstrap SE of 0.293, the approximate minimum detectable effect for a two-sided 5% test at 80% power is 2.80 × SE ≈ 0.82 bps per SD of flow per SD of skew.

8. Robustness

Of 13 prespecified checks with intervals, 13 share the sign of the primary estimate and 0 exclude zero (Table 7, Figure 5). The checks cover:

  • the SVI method, with both common-observation and separate-coverage estimates;
  • quote filters at 60 or 180 s record age and 0.25 or 1.00 relative spread;
  • delayed endpoints and a 10 s flow-gap rule;
  • exclusion of the two final-period roll days.

All differ from the primary by at most 0.073 bps, with similar interval widths. Calendar-month estimates (no intervals; small samples) range from -0.82 to 0.99 and change sign. This is what sampling noise looks like, not a stable regime effect. Robustness results are not treated as new chances to find a headline.

The placebo estimate is 0.265 (CI [-0.096, 0.520], p = 0.093). It is no smaller than the primary estimate, which is consistent with the primary estimate reflecting noise rather than same-day state dependence. The placebo substitutes the state from five eligible sessions earlier at the same session minute. This breaks same-day alignment between skew and flow while preserving the slow-moving skew level and intraday seasonality.

The pretest-defined skew groups (20th and 80th percentiles) contain 26 (low) and 17 (high) final sessions. Within them, descriptive selling-pressure slopes are 0.012 and 0.110 bps per SD. These are descriptive only; the continuous interaction remains primary.

9. Discussion

What the null rules out. The interval excludes interactions larger than about 0.61 bps per SD² in either direction. A one-SD selling-pressure minute in a high-skew state (z = +1) is followed by a five-minute return that differs from a low-skew state (z = −1) by 0.23 bps (CI [-1.15, 1.22]). That is small next to the 6.91 bps standard deviation of five-minute returns. An interaction large enough that a one-SD rise in skew shifts the five-minute response to a one-SD selling minute by more than about 0.61 bps is inconsistent with these data. Effects below roughly 0.82 bps per SD² cannot be resolved.

Why the null is plausible.

  • Fast absorption. At five minutes the ES market is very liquid. Most price adjustment to flow is contemporaneous (correlation 0.80), leaving little forward drift for any state to modulate.
  • Skew is slow-moving. Only 2.9% of the variance of five-minute SK30 states is within-day; the rest is between days (Figure 6). Daily mean SK30 and ATM IV correlate at 0.93. Much of skew's information is about regimes already reflected in ATM IV, realized volatility and depth, all of which are controlled.
  • Sampled flow. Second-level sampling attenuates flow variation (SD ratio 0.63 on the audit day) and adds measurement error, which biases interaction estimates toward zero and could hide sub-second effects. With a per-minute correlation of 0.92 between sampled and event-level OFI, the attenuation is likely modest. It cannot plausibly hide an effect several times larger than the interval bounds, but it does weaken the claim for small effects.
  • Earlier evidence is daily. The daily-frequency link between option-implied crash risk and illiquidity (Christoffersen et al. 2021) need not imply an intraday conditional-response effect.

Measurement. The result does not hinge on surface construction (A versus B correlation 0.998; nearly identical estimates) or on quote filters. Quote-perturbation standard deviations (at most 0.11 vol points) are small next to the pretest skew SD of 2.31 vol points. Selection does not run toward stress: the usable share is higher in high-volatility states.

10. Limitations

  • Order flow is a 1-second sampled best-quote imbalance, not event-level OFI (D-014).
  • One market pair and one year; the final period is four months (85 sessions).
  • Skew is partly a volatility-regime variable; ATM IV and realized-volatility controls address this only in part.
  • SPXW and ES timestamps come from different collection locations; the deliberate lag guards against look-ahead, not every cross-market timing artifact.
  • Over half of SVI fits violate a finite-grid slope condition in the far upside wing; Method B is a robustness check, not an arbitrage-free surface.
  • The frozen expiry instant ignores half-day early expirations (measured effect is small; Section 4.6).
  • The last observation of each session is dropped at h = 15 by an over-conservative endpoint mask (1 of 366 rows per session; secondary horizon only; D-024).
  • Midquote returns are not tradable P&L, and no causal mechanism is identified.
  • The literature review is verified at the bibliographic and abstract level only.
  • The pipeline was tested on Windows only.

11. Conclusion

The 95% block-bootstrap interval includes zero, so the primary null of no sell-pressure × skew interaction is not rejected at the 5% level. The result is inconclusive about the interaction, not evidence that it is exactly zero. The interaction is small relative to return dispersion and is stable across surface methods, quote filters and endpoint definitions. It adds no out-of-sample forecasting value. Within the stated precision, the price of S&P 500 downside protection does not change how E-mini prices continue or reverse after observed selling pressure over the next one to fifteen minutes. The locked, budgeted and fully logged pipeline makes it straightforward to revisit the question with event-level order flow, a longer sample or another market pair.

Technical appendix

A. Cash-settled European parity and normalization

For a European, cash-settled call and put with strike K and the same expiry T, the payoff difference is (S_T − K)⁺ − (K − S_T)⁺ = S_T − K. Under any pricing measure with discount factor D = P(0, T) for cash paid at T, and forward F = E[S_T] for the index level at T,

C(K) − P(K) = D·(F − K) = A − B·K, with A = D·F and B = D, so F = A/B.

Neither D nor F is assumed: both come from the cross-section of matched strikes at each cutoff. Dividends, financing and settlement conventions are absorbed into F and D for that expiry, so the ES price, which has a different maturity, is never used as the forward.

Normalization. Define x = K/F, c = C/A and p = P/A. Then c − p = 1 − x. Multiplying every premium of an expiry by a common positive constant λ (for example, converting index points to dollars with the multiplier) multiplies A and B by λ, leaves F = A/B unchanged and leaves c, p, x unchanged. Implied volatilities and skew are therefore invariant to the unit of premium; unit tests verify this. Mixing units (dollar premiums with index-point strikes) is rejected because the implied rate leaves the guardrail.

B. Black normalized pricing, implied variance and delta

With total variance w = σ²τ, c(x, w) = Φ(d₁) − xΦ(d₂), d₁ = (−ln x + w/2)/√w, d₂ = d₁ − √w. c is strictly increasing in w on (max(1 − x, 0), 1). The implied w is found by bracketed root search (Brent; a vectorized bisection on ln w for grids). Prices outside the open bounds are rejected, never clamped. τ is ACT/365 elapsed time from the cutoff to the expiry instant. The quoting delta is the undiscounted forward delta Φ(d₁) (call) and Φ(d₁) − 1 (put). The 25-delta put is where the call delta equals 0.75.

C. Method A quadratic program

At distinct observed strike knots x₁ < … < x_m with fitted values c_j and slopes s_j = (c_{j+1} − c_j)/(x_{j+1} − x_j):

minimize (1/n)Σ_i ((c(x_i) − c_mid,i)/h_i)² + 100·(1/n)Σ_i ((c(x_i) − c_hi,i)⁺ + (c_lo,i − c(x_i))⁺)²/h_i² + λ·Σ_j (s_{j+1} − s_j)²/Δx̄_j

subject to max(1 − x_j, 0) ≤ c_j ≤ 1, −1 ≤ s_j ≤ 0, s_{j+1} ≥ s_j,

where i runs over all call and put observations (puts converted by parity), h_i = max(half-spread_i, 2×10⁻⁵) and Δx̄j = (x{j+2} − x_j)/2. Between knots the fitted call is linear, so the constraints hold on the whole observed support. The problem is solved with CLARABEL via CVXPY. The 30-day curve is c₃₀(x) = a·c₁(x) + (1 − a)·c₂(x) with a = (τ₂ − τ₃₀)/(τ₂ − τ₁). This is a convex combination, which preserves the strike constraints on common support. λ selection uses five strike folds per expiry (strike rank mod 5); held-out quotes inside the training support are scored. D. Method B raw SVI

Parameters (a, b, ρ, m, σ) are bounded to a in [−0.5, 1], b in [10⁻⁴, 5], ρ in [−0.999, 0.999], m in [−1, 1] and σ in [10⁻⁴, 2]. Initialization is deterministic: a 12-point grid least-squares fit to implied total variance of mid quotes, followed by premium-space refinement with the spread-normalized loss (warm-started from the same expiry's previous fit within the session). The checks are: positive w, call bounds, slopes in [−1, 0] and convexity on a 401-point grid inside support. 30-day interpolation is linear in total variance at fixed k. Failing checks are flagged and reported (Section 4.4).

E. Order-flow construction details

ES bbo-1s records give the BBO state at the end of each printed one-second interval. Records are valid if both sides are finite, ask > bid, sizes are positive and no bad-timestamp or bad-book flag is set. The increment of Section 5.1 is computed between consecutive valid records within a segment. Depth is the time-weighted mean of (Q_b + Q_a)/2 over the 60 one-second states in the window. Hand-computed unit tests cover same-price size changes, moving prices, duplicated states, multi-record events (collapsing on F_LAST for event-level data), invalid states and resets.

H. Bootstrap algorithm and tests

  1. List the G = 85 scheduled final sessions in calendar order, including any without valid rows.
  2. For b = 1, …, 1999 (seed 1729): draw ceil(G/5) block starts uniformly from the G − 4 possible starts, and concatenate the five consecutive session positions of each block, truncated to G.
  3. Collect every original observation of each drawn session, relabelling repeated copies. Returns are never recomputed across block boundaries.
  4. Refit the frozen M_SKEW regression to get θ*_b.
  5. Report θ_hat, the basic interval [2θ_hat − q(0.975), 2θ_hat − q(0.025)] of the draws, the percentile interval, and the null-centered p = (1 + #{|θ*_b − θ_hat| ≥ |θ_hat|})/(B + 1).

The secondary family (b·z and s·z at 1, 2, 5, 10, 15 minutes, excluding the primary) is Holm-adjusted. Forecast comparisons resample paired daily blocks of losses. Synthetic checks (labelled SYNTHETIC): null rejection 6.0% (Monte Carlo SE 1.7%, 200 replications); power 100.0% at δ = −0.25.
